How does an intention become a habit?

A habit grows through repetition, not willpower. You do not have to be perfect: keep coming back to it.

1. Make a concrete plan: which day, when, how long, what.
2. Start smaller than you think. A short session counts.
3. Repeat in the same situation. It will start reminding you.
4. Record what happened: a mirror, not a grade.
5. Notice what feels good. Enjoyment brings you back.
6. After a gap, restart without shame. Returning matters.
7. Do it together. Look out for someone and let someone look out for you.
8. Prepare your surroundings. That part happens outside the app.

What is a habit?
An action that a familiar situation starts almost automatically.

At first, every session needs a decision. Repeating it in a similar situation, such as after morning coffee, makes that situation a cue, so starting takes less effort.

This takes time and varies greatly. In one study, a daily action approached automaticity after a median of 66 days, with individual estimates ranging from 18 to 254 days. Simple actions formed faster than complex ones [1]. A promise of “21 days” misses this variation.

In the app: Progress shows the weeks in which you made time for your habit. It does not count a daily streak or punish a missed day.

Plan when, how long and what.
A concrete plan works better than an intention.

“I will move more” does not say when to start. “On Wednesday after work, I will walk for 20 minutes” does. These if–then plans helped people turn intentions into action across 94 studies [2].

Attach the session to an existing routine: after brushing your teeth, arriving home or having coffee. A study of flossing after toothbrushing illustrates this approach [3].

In the app: choose the day, part of day or exact time, duration and activity. Picture yourself in that situation.

Start smaller than you think.
Ten minutes you do are worth more than sixty you do not.

We tend to underestimate the time and energy plans require: the planning fallacy [4]. An oversized plan can lead to disappointment and giving up. Repetition, not duration, is the basis of a habit [1,5].

Plan two or three short sessions in the first weeks. If you enjoy one, keep going: the plan is a minimum, not a ceiling. A shorter session still counts.

In the app: Progress may suggest a more realistic plan if actual time is repeatedly below planned time. You decide.

You decide.
Your own choice sustains you better than pressure.

Persistence is supported by choosing for ourselves, feeling capable and connecting with others [6]. Supportive environments that respect choice are associated with better outcomes in health contexts too [7].

Choose something you enjoy a little. Change activities if you are bored; the habit is making regular time. Pause when life does not allow it.

In the app: choose and change activities, decide whether result details are private or shared with your group, and pause or close a habit without losing its history.

Record what happened.
Recording is a mirror, not a grade.

A review of 138 experiments found that monitoring progress helped goal attainment, especially when people wrote down or shared results [8].

Honesty matters more than attractive numbers. Record a shorter session as shorter. A session that did not happen can suggest a poor time slot or an oversized plan.

In the app: record actual time, day, activity and enjoyment. You can catch up until the deadline. A missing result is unknown, not a failure.

Notice what feels good.
Immediate enjoyment brings you back more than a distant goal.

Long-term goals matter, but enjoyment during the activity can support persistence [9]. Fresh air, conversation, music or the feeling of having done it can help.

Pair the activity with something you enjoy, such as a favourite podcast only on walks. A study of this kind of “temptation bundling” increased gym attendance [10].

In the app: rate how it felt. Progress helps you notice enjoyable activities and times.

See your rhythm.
Each session is evidence that this is part of you.

Over time, “I ran today” may become “I am a runner”. Habit and identity are related and can reinforce each other [11]. A perfect streak is unnecessary; regularly making room for the habit is enough.

In the app: Progress shows weekly rhythm, active weeks, time spent and returns. No rankings or comparison with other people: your story is the reference.

After a gap, restart without shame.
A missed session does not erase what you have built.

Illness, work, children and travel interrupt life. One missed opportunity did not materially affect habit formation in the study above [1]. The “what the hell” response can be more harmful: one slip can lead to giving up [12].

Treat yourself like a friend. Self-compassion can support efforts to improve [13]; shame tends to promote avoidance [14]. Do not compensate with a double session: make the next one smaller. A new week, month or group round can help you restart [15].

In the app: Returns is a key Progress measure. “Did not happen” is neutral; there is no streak to lose. Pause the habit when necessary.

It is easier together.
Knowing someone counts on you can help you start.

Social support helps. In a weight-loss study, participants who joined with friends and received support maintained outcomes more often than those joining alone [16]. Connection also supports motivation [6].

When everyone is responsible, everyone may wait for someone else [17]. Here, one person looks out for you, and you look out for someone.

In the app: “Let’s do it together” starts a free pair round from an existing habit. In larger groups, pairings rotate. You see plans and participation; each person chooses whether to share details.

Responsibility for someone, and someone for you.
Supporting someone else can give you strength too.

Providing support can offer meaning and energy. Observational research associates giving support with the supporter’s own health, without proving causation [18].

Ask rather than police. Respecting choice is more helpful than judgement [7]. Try “When would a short session fit?” or “How are you? Can I help?” rather than “You missed it again” or “Everyone else did it”.

In the app: a missing plan or late result triggers one prompt to check in. Send appreciation, encouragement or a playful nudge. There are no rankings or public shaming.

When you are captain.
You are not a coach: help ensure nobody is overlooked.

In groups of three or more, a different person is captain each week. If support is missing, remind the person responsible. You do not contact the supported person instead or tell them what to do.

Rotation lets everyone experience being counted on and being supported.

In the app: your first captain week explains what you see and when you receive a task. Each task states its reason and how to close it.

What you can do outside the app.
Visible, accessible things are easier to do.

Surroundings strongly influence habits [5,19]. Prepare shoes, a bag or a mat the evening before. Keep a book on your pillow, an instrument on a stand or flashcards by the coffee maker. Reduce steps needed to start. Put distractions in another room. Repeat in the same place and time [3,19]. Tell someone your intention in person.

Sleep and food matter too. When you are tired or hungry, the smallest version is enough. The app cannot arrange your environment for you.

1. On your own, with one habit
If you want to try quietly first.

Free, even as a guest without registration. Choose one habit and plan two or three short sessions. Record what happened; look at Progress after two or three weeks.

When ready, invite someone with “Let’s do it together”. Link the existing habit to the group so future sessions count in both and your personal history remains intact.

2. As a pair
If there is one person you want to do it with.

Pair rounds are always free. You look out for each other; a missing plan or late result gives the other person one prompt to check in.

You need not do the same activity: one can run and the other swim within the same category. You can invite a third person later.

3. In a group of 3–8
If you already have a team.

Friends, colleagues, training partners or reading and language groups can start a 4-, 8- or 12-week round on Monday. The warm-up week lets you begin together beforehand. Pairings and the captain rotate weekly.

The creator’s first group has a four-week free trial. Later, one member pays per round; participation is free for the others. If unsure, start alone for a week or two, then invite one person.

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Ask your doctor if you are unsure about an activity because of your health.
